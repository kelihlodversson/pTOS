pTOS - address spaces on 32-bit ARM and m68k with an MMU
==================================================


The layout the MMU configurations of ARM (ARMv6/ARMv7) and m68k
(68030/68040/68060) move to under #549: one address space per process, and a
higher-half kernel.  It follows the x86-64/x32 model of
doc/x86_64-address-space.txt; where nothing is said here, that document's
rules apply unchanged, with one exception: the 68030/68040/68060 MMUs have
supervisor-only and write-protect bits but no execute-disable bit, so on
m68k there is no non-executable data or stack, and aspace_translate()
reports PROT_EXEC for every readable page.  (ARM's XN bit has it.)  Nothing
in this file applies to the configurations
without an MMU, the m68k ones without a PMMU included: they keep the flat TOS
memory model, and the kernel stays where it is linked today.

Every later step of #549 refers to this file: #562 (the procmem.h interface),
#563 (no kernel pointers in user-visible results), the higher-half kernels
(#566, #567, #569) and the address spaces themselves (#568, #570, #571).


The split
----------

Both architectures split the 32-bit address space in half:

    0x00000000 - 0x7fffffff   user half: the process's own, one per process
    0x80000000 - 0xffffffff   kernel half: the same in every process,
                              supervisor-only

The half split is not arbitrary:

  - Every user address is a positive signed LONG and every kernel address a
    negative one.  The x32 user layout already lies below 2 GiB for this
    reason: TOS software tells pointers from error codes by the sign.  It
    also makes the rule of #563 checkable: a negative value that is not an
    error code and reaches a user is a kernel pointer that leaked.
  - ARM: TTBCR.N = 1 makes TTBR0 translate exactly the lower 2 GiB and
    TTBR1 the upper 2 GiB, so the user table is half the size (8 KiB of
    first-level table) and the kernel table is never touched on a switch.
  - m68k: the root table of the 68040/68060 has 128 entries of 32 MiB each;
    entries 0-63 are the user half and 64-127 the kernel half, so a process
    root is the shared kernel half plus its own 64 entries.
  - The same user layout as x32 fits (below), so procmem.h and the BDOS code
    built on it need different types (#562), not different addresses.

3 GiB/1 GiB was considered and rejected: user addresses above 2 GiB are
negative LONGs, which breaks the rule above for user pointers, and the gain
is address space no TOS program can use safely.


The kernel half
----------------

    0x80000000 - 0xbfffffff   the physical-memory window (1 GiB)
    0xc0000000 - 0xffffffff   devices (1 GiB)

The physical-memory window maps the machine's RAM from its first byte:

    virtual = physical - RAM_BASE + 0x80000000

where RAM_BASE is where the RAM starts on the machine: 0 on the Raspberry
Pi, virt-m68k and the Atari, 0x40000000 on virt-arm.  It plays the part of
x86-64's physical direct map, and holds everything of the kernel:

  - the kernel image itself.  It stays where it is loaded in physical
    memory and is linked at its window address, so translating between a
    kernel address and a physical one is one subtraction (virt_to_phys(),
    phys_to_virt()).  A ROM kernel (the Atari ROM images, #571) is reached
    the same way: the ROM at physical 0x00e00000 is at 0x80e00000.
  - its data, bss and stacks, the same way.
  - the kernel heap (#362) and the per-process kernel stacks.  They come
    from the physical page allocator and are used through the window, as
    x32's kernel stacks are; no separate area for them is needed.
  - the page tables, and the physical pages of every process.  The kernel
    copies to and from user memory through the window after it has checked
    the range (kproc_copy_from_user() and friends), so the copy cannot fault
    and works whichever address space is loaded.

1 GiB is the most RAM the MMU configurations use (decided in review).  That is all the RAM of
every Raspberry Pi up to the 3B+, the most virt-arm uses today
(VIRT_MAX_RAM_SIZE), and more than any Atari has.  virt-m68k today accepts
up to just under 2 GiB, and a Raspberry Pi 4 may have more than 1 GiB; in
the MMU configurations the RAM above 1 GiB is left unused, as LPAE
(#76) and a high-memory scheme are out of scope.

The device window holds the memory-mapped I/O, supervisor-only, uncached:

  virt-arm    as today (#508): hardware 0x00000000-0x0fffffff at
              0xf0000000, and the PCIe windows at 0x10000000-0x3fffffff
              at 0xc0000000 (VIRT_DEV_VA()).
  Raspberry   virtual = 0xf0000000 | (physical & 0x0fffffff) for the
  Pi          peripherals and the ARM-local block.  That puts the BCM2835
              peripherals (0x20000000) at 0xf0000000, the BCM2836/7 ones
              (0x3f000000) at 0xff000000 with the local block (0x40000000)
              at 0xf0000000, and the BCM2711 ones (0xfe000000, GIC at
              0xff840000) at their own addresses.  The PCIe window of the
              Pi 4 lies above 4 GiB and stays out of reach (#76, #276).
  virt-m68k   the goldfish and virtio devices at 0xff000000 and up, at
              their own addresses.
  Atari       the I/O area at 0xffff8000-0xffffffff, at its own address,
              which is what EmuTOS's short absolute addressing ($8240.w)
              already uses, and the IDE interface, which the code reaches
              at 0xfff00000-0xffffffff (bios/ide.c).  That whole range
              is an alias of physical 0x00f00000-0x00ffffff, so the two
              addresses differ: virtual 0xfff00000 is physical
              0x00f00000, as bios/arch/m68k/68040_pmmu.S maps it today.
              The cartridge and ROM areas are reached through the
              physical-memory window.

The window and the device mappings are global (not tied to a process): ARM
first-level entries without nG, 68040/68060 descriptors with G set, so a
switch never flushes them.


The user half
--------------

The user half is laid out exactly like x32's (the constants of
include/procmem.h; #562 gives them 32-bit types):

    0x00000000 - 0x001fffff   kernel low mappings and null guard (below)
    0x00200000 - 0x003fffff   basepage, environment and TPA blocks (window)
    0x00400000 - 0x007fffff   program image, and the startup area after it
    0x0fff0000 - 0x0fff0fff   the ancestors' basepages, read-only
    0x10000000 - 0x7ffbefff   Malloc() memory
    0x7ffbf000 - 0x7ffbffff   guard page
    0x7ffc0000 - 0x7fffffff   user stack (256 KiB), growing down from
                              0x80000000

The last three lines are the defaults.  As on x32, the guard page sits
immediately below the stack and the Malloc() memory ends below it, so
Psetlimit() of the stack size moves all three: a 1 MiB stack starts at
0x7ff00000, with the guard page below it.  (x32's 16 MiB default puts them
at 0x7efff000 and 0x7f000000.)

The same rules hold as on x32, except that nothing is backed when touched:
the first ARM and m68k backends have no demand paging (#549 leaves it out,
CONF_WITH_DEMAND_PAGING, #562).  Malloc() memory is mapped when a block is
handed out, and the stack when the process starts, 256 KiB by default
(USER_STACK_SIZE) instead of x32's 16 MiB; Psetlimit() maps a bigger one at
once.  Malloc(-1) reports no more than there is physical memory for.  The
process window (basepage, environment,
TPA) holds blocks the BDOS reaches by the same address in every address
space, so its addresses are unique across processes and are never reused
while a block is live.  Everything else is private: the image, the startup
area, the Malloc() memory and the stack have the same virtual addresses in
every process, and different physical pages behind them.

Kernel low mappings.  The lowest 2 MiB is not mapped for the process.

  m68k: the exception vectors (0x000-0x3ff, at VBR 0) and the TOS system
  variables (0x380-0x7ff), and the low ST-RAM sections that end at
  __low_stram_end (at most 32 KiB, emutos.ld asserts it), are mapped at
  their own addresses, supervisor-only, in every process, because the BDOS
  and the BIOS reach them there while they serve the process's calls.
  This is x32's "kernel low mappings".  A user-mode access faults, as it
  does on a real ST, where the GLUE protects the first 2 KiB.  The rest of
  the 2 MiB is the null guard.

  ARM: nothing is mapped there.  The system variables are ordinary kernel
  variables on ARM (#219, bios/tosvars.c).  The native exception table moves
  into the kernel image through VBAR, which the ARM1176 of the Pi 1 has as
  well as every ARMv7 core.  The emulated TOS vector table does not: it
  occupies 0x8-0x3ff today (bios/arch/arm/vectors.c initialises it, and the
  startup dispatchers read their handlers there).  Before the low 2 MiB can
  be left unmapped, that table has to move into the kernel image, and its
  writers (vectors.c, Setexc()) and readers (the dispatchers) with it.  This
  is a prerequisite of #566 and #567.  The whole 2 MiB is then the null
  guard.

What a user program may read in low memory: nothing.  A process stays in
user mode (below), and reads the system variables it needs through
Ssystem(), the documented way, which works from user mode (#221, #237).


Super() and Supexec()
----------------------

A process in supervisor mode would see the whole kernel half: the
supervisor-only bit is exactly what it gets past.  So in the MMU
configurations no process gets there:

  - Super() returns EACCDN and leaves the process in user mode.  The query
    form, Super(1), still answers, with 0: the caller is in user mode.
  - Supexec() returns EACCDN without calling the function.

EACCDN (access denied), not EINVFN: the call exists, the configuration
refuses it.  Programs that need supervisor mode -- for the system variables,
the hardware registers or the screen -- do not run in the MMU
configurations; they run in the flat configurations, which keep both calls
as they are.

This is m68k's rule.  On ARM, Supexec() already returns EINVFN in every
configuration (#219, bios/xbios.c), deprecated for Ssystem(), and the ARM
MMU configurations keep that: nothing reinstates it, and Super() does not
reach the system variables, which are not at fixed addresses there.  Only
Super() is new on ARM, and it returns EACCDN as on m68k.


The screen
-----------

The screen memory is kernel memory, in the physical-memory window, and is
not mapped for any process.  Because the screen's address is a kernel
address, and no kernel address may reach a user as a LONG (below, #563),
Logbase() and Physbase() return 0 in the MMU configurations.

A user-mode program cannot draw through the VDI or the AES either, for now.
As on x32 (doc/x86_64-address-space.txt, #376), calls to them from user mode
are refused: the VDI reads bitmap descriptors (MFDB) and buffers by the
program's own pointers (vdi/vdi_raster.c), and neither it nor the AES checks
them against the calling process.  A validated GEM entry path, with the
nested buffers checked, is a prerequisite of any user-mode drawing and is
the work of epic #548.  The first MMU configurations are therefore the
command-line ones (virt-arm-cli, virt-m68k-cli, and the like); the desktop
runs in the configurations without per-process address spaces.

Mapping the screen for a process (at a fixed user address, read/write, and
then returned as that address) is left for later, if programs that draw
into it directly are to run in the MMU configurations.


Page tables and switching
--------------------------

ARM.  TTBCR.N = 1.  TTBR1 holds the kernel's first-level table (16 KiB, of
which only the upper half is used) for the whole uptime; TTBR0 holds the
running process's first-level table (8 KiB, 2048 entries of 1 MiB) with its
second-level tables (1 KiB each, 256 pages of 4 KiB).  The format is the
ARMv6 extended one the ports already use (XP set): APX/AP give the
permissions, XN marks every data page non-executable, and nG marks every
user mapping.  A switch writes TTBR0 and CONTEXTIDR.

  ASIDs: switches only happen at Pexec(), Pterm() and the return to a
  launcher, so the first version uses no ASID allocation: every process
  runs with ASID 0 and a switch invalidates the TLB entries of that ASID
  (TLBIASID, which the ARM1176 has as "invalidate by ASID match" as well as
  the ARMv7 cores).  TLBIALL is not used, because it would also drop the
  global kernel entries that the guarantee above says survive a switch.
  Using the 256 ASIDs can come later, if multitasking makes switches
  frequent.

  Caches: the L1 caches of the ARM1176 and of the ARMv7 cores are virtually
  indexed and physically tagged.  Two processes that map different physical
  pages at the same virtual address therefore never hit each other's lines,
  and the ARM1176's 16 KiB 4-way caches have 4 KiB ways, so they do not
  alias with 4 KiB pages either.  A switch needs no cache maintenance, only
  the TLB.  What does need it is loading code: the loader writes a program's
  image through the data cache, so it cleans the data cache and invalidates
  the instruction cache (with the barrier) over the pages before the
  process first runs, as the ELF loader must already do for any program.

m68k, 68040/68060.  The MMU has two root pointers, URP for user accesses
and SRP for supervisor ones.  Both point to the running process's root
table (128 longwords): its upper 64 entries are the same pointers in every
process, to the kernel's second-level tables, and the lower 64 its own.
Using the same root for both is what lets the BDOS, in supervisor mode,
reach the process's buffers by their user addresses, as x86-64 does; the S
bit in the page descriptors keeps user mode out of the kernel pages.  The
page size is 4 KiB (TC.P = 0), the same as ARM's and x32's.  A switch loads
URP and SRP and flushes the non-global ATC entries (PFLUSHAN); the kernel's
descriptors have G set and stay.  The 68040/68060 caches are physically
tagged, so a switch needs no cache flush.

m68k, 68030.  It is done after the 68040/68060, in #571, for the TT and
the Falcon (a 68EC030 is refused at boot, #564).  The plan (decided in
review):

  - The same tree shape as the 68040's, so one piece of code builds both:
    4 KiB pages and TC with PS = 12, IS = 0, TIA = 7, TIB = 7, TIC = 6, which
    makes a root of 128 entries of 32 MiB, user entries 0-63 and kernel
    entries 64-127, as above.
  - Long-format descriptors (8 bytes), because only they have the S bit
    that makes a page supervisor-only, and WP for read-only pages.  The
    tables are twice the 68040's size (a 1 KiB root).
  - RAM is cached write-through: the 68030's data cache is always
    write-through (bios/arch/m68k/processor.S says so), so the tree has no
    copyback mode to ask for; the 68040/68060 trees use copyback for RAM.
  - CRP for both modes (TC.SRE clear), so supervisor accesses use the
    process's tree too and the BDOS reaches the process's buffers by
    their user addresses, as on the 68040.  SRP is not used.
  - The I/O area through page descriptors, not a transparent translation:
    a TT only passes an address through, and the Atari I/O window is an
    alias (virtual 0xfff00000 is physical 0x00f00000, above).  The tree maps
    it the way bios/pmmu030.c does today (its tables for 0xfff00000 and up),
    supervisor-only and cache inhibited, outside the TT ranges that
    bios/arch/m68k/processor.S sets up.  A user-mode access finds the S bit
    and faults.  The tree itself replaces the PMMU tree pTOS builds today for
    the caches (CONF_WITH_68030_PMMU), which is not per process.
  - TT0 and TT1 are disabled in the MMU configuration.  Today
    processor_init() (bios/arch/m68k/processor.S, init_ttr0/init_ttr1) turns
    on identity translations for all function codes over 0x01000000-
    0x7fffffff (TT-RAM) and 0x80000000-0xfeffffff.  Those bypass the tree,
    which would defeat the private pages of the user half and send the
    kernel window to the wrong physical addresses.  The MMU configuration
    clears both registers and processor_init() must not restore them; the
    tree gives every range its cache mode instead (copyback or write-through
    for RAM, cache inhibited for I/O).  TT-RAM is part of the physical-memory
    window, up to the 1 GiB limit.
  - A switch loads CRP, which also flushes the ATC; the 68030 has no
    global bit, so the kernel entries are reloaded as they are used.  Its
    caches are logically addressed, so the instruction and data caches are
    invalidated too.  The data cache is write-through, so nothing is lost,
    and refilling 2 x 256 bytes is cheap.
  - The ROM, at physical 0x00e00000, runs from 0x80e00000 in the window.
    The screen base, the floppy and ACSI/SCSI DMA take physical addresses
    (virt_to_phys()); TT-RAM stays non-DMA as today.
  - Tested under Hatari, whose 68030 has an MMU (--mmu on, TT and Falcon),
    with the regression tests of tools/run-hatari-tests.py; QEMU emulates
    no 68030 MMU.

QEMU emulates the MMU of the 68040 only, so virt-m68k with -cpu m68040 is
the test machine for m68k (#569, #570); the 68060 and 68030 code is
checked on hardware or in another emulator.


Starting with the MMU off
--------------------------

A higher-half kernel starts at a low physical address with the MMU off, as
on x86-64.  A short position-independent start builds the first tables
(the window, the devices, and an identity mapping of the start code
itself), switches the MMU on, jumps to the window address, and removes the
identity mapping once nothing runs there.  ARM: virt_mmu_bootstrap() and
the raspi startup become this.  m68k: the classic ROM startup never
switches the PMMU on before the BIOS runs; the MMU configurations do, in
startup.S, right after the PMMU check of #564.

Legacy m68k initialisation must not undo the early mapping.  bios_init()
calls processor_init(), whose 68040/68060 path disables translation (TC) and
installs transparent mappings for all addresses
(bios/arch/m68k/processor.S, ITT0/DTT0/DTT1), and the later
setup_68040_pmmu()/setup_68030_pmmu() calls (CONF_WITH_68040_PMMU,
CONF_WITH_68030_PMMU) disable translation and replace the tree.  Clearing TC
while running at a higher-half address loses the kernel's own mapping.  In
the MMU configurations the early start owns the MMU: processor_init() skips
the TC/TT part (it only does its CPU and cache detection), and the legacy
tree builders are not used, their job being done by the per-process trees.
The flat configurations keep both paths exactly as they are.

Code that hands an address to hardware (DMA, the screen base, the USB and
eMMC controllers, virtio queues) converts it with virt_to_phys().  The
flat configurations keep the translation they have: the identity where the
RAM is mapped at its physical address (the Atari, virt-m68k, the Raspberry
Pi), and virt-arm's existing offset, where the RAM is mapped at virtual 0
while it is physically at VIRT_RAM_BASE (bios/machine/virt-arm/virt_mmu.c).
The MMU configurations translate by where the address is:

  - an address in the physical-memory window: the subtraction above,
    physical = virtual - 0x80000000 + RAM_BASE;
  - an address in the low kernel mappings (m68k: the vectors, the system
    variables and the low ST-RAM up to __low_stram_end): the identity,
    because those pages are mapped at their own addresses.  The disk
    buffer (dskbuf, bios/lowstram.c) lives there and is handed to disk I/O
    by that address, so the subtraction would send the DMA to
    0x80004000 for 0x4000;
  - anything else is not memory the hardware can be given.

The same memory is also in the window, so a caller that can choose passes
the window address.

virt-arm, as built by #566 (CONF_WITH_HIGHER_HALF)
...................................................

The first higher-half kernel.  What it does, and where it stops short of the
end state above, until #568 gives each process a table of its own:

  - The kernel is linked at 0x80000000 and up (emutos.ld, MEMORY stram), the
    RAM's first byte being at 0x80000000, and loaded where the RAM is
    (LMA = VMA - 0x40000000, so QEMU's -kernel loader needs no change).  The
    code before the MMU is on (startup.S up to the jump, virt_uart.c,
    virt_mmu_bootstrap()) runs at the physical address and is position
    independent: no symbol address, no global.
  - TTBCR.N = 1.  TTBR1 holds the kernel half for the whole uptime: the
    window (RAM, 1 MiB sections) and the devices (as before, VIRT_DEV_VA()).
    TTBR0 holds the lower half: the RAM again at virtual 0, which is the pool
    the memory of the programs comes from, and the first 16 MiB of the RAM at
    its physical address, for the MMU switches at boot and reset (kept).  The
    pool is the memory after the kernel image (bmem_init() converts the end of
    the kernel with virt_pool_start()), so Malloc(), Pexec() and every other
    address given to a program stays a positive LONG, and phystop, membot and
    memtop are pool addresses.  The pool is a second view of the same
    physical memory, with the same attributes.
  - The kernel half is not supervisor-only yet.  The command line and the
    desktop are part of the kernel image and run in user mode, so they have to
    execute and read the kernel's text and data in the window.  Making the
    half supervisor-only is part of #568, with the programs that run from
    their own memory.
  - No fixed low addresses, and no emulated vector table: the ARM exception
    handlers (startup.S) dispatch to a few ordinary variables --
    tos_vec_prefetch, tos_vec_data, tos_vec_undef, tos_vec_linea and
    tos_vec_svc[16] (bios/arch/arm/vectors.c) -- and Setexc() of the vector
    numbers they stand for (0x02, 0x03, 0x04, 0x0a and 0x20-0x2f) reads and
    writes them; any other vector does not exist on ARM, so Setexc() of it
    returns 0 and sets nothing.  The crash page (proc_lives and the others)
    is made of ordinary variables.  The native exception table is installed
    with VBAR as before.
  - virtio DMA addresses are translated by where the address is
    (virt_to_phys(), VIRTIO_PHYS() in util/virtio.h): the window or the pool.
  - A message id of the NLS (NLS_MSGID_FLAG) is no longer the top bit alone,
    which a string of the kernel image has now.


Falcon (68030), as built by #726 (CONF_ATARI_HIGHER_HALF)
..........................................................

falcon-mmu_defconfig is a 512 KB ROM that runs in the window with the 68030
MMU on from its first instruction.  What is different from the plan above and
from virt-m68k below:

  - The tree is a static one in the ROM (bios/arch/m68k/atari_mmu.S), loaded
    by the first instructions of startup.S, before the RAM is configured:
    on a Falcon the memory controller is set up by the ROM (memory.S), so
    there is no RAM to build a tree in yet.  The descriptors have U and M
    set already, so the MMU never writes one back into the ROM.  The shape
    is the plan's (4 KiB pages, TC PS = 12, IS = 0, TIA = 7, TIB = 7, TIC = 6,
    long-format descriptors, CRP only) but most of it is early termination: a
    descriptor of the root (32 MiB) or of the second level (256 KiB) maps its
    whole range, and only the first 256 KiB are split into pages, for the
    supervisor-only first page.  The per-process trees of #727 are built in
    RAM later and replace the user half; this one is the boot tree (and the
    one a software reset comes back to).
  - The ROM is linked at 0x80e00000 and loaded at 0x00e00000 (LMA = VMA -
    0x80000000).  Its header, which Hatari and the hardware read, is
    physical: the reset vector and os_beg are the physical addresses.  .data
    and .bss are in the window, right after the TOS low memory, which is at
    its fixed addresses as on virt-m68k.
  - The window maps the physical memory from 0, ST-RAM, ROM and TT-RAM
    (0x80000000-0x9fffffff); the same memory is at its physical addresses, which
    is where the pool the programs live in, the screen and the DMA buffers
    are.  The I/O area (0x00f00000-0x00ffffff and the alias 0xff000000 up that
    code reaches it at, 0xffff8000 and so on) is supervisor-only and not
    cached.  The old tree of bios/pmmu030.c, the TT0/TT1 of processor_init()
    and the 68040 tree are not installed in this configuration.
  - A DMA address and the video base are physical: set_dma_addr() and
    atari_setphys() translate the address of a buffer of the kernel image
    (virt_to_phys(), include/atari_mmu.h).
  - The image is for the 68030 only: the 68040 and 68060 have another MMU
    (#728), and the ROM does not check for the CPU before it uses the MMU.
  - As on virt-m68k-mmu, nothing hides the kernel's addresses from a program
    yet: without CONF_WITH_PROCESS_ASPACES the rules of doc/kernel-pointers.txt
    do not apply, and what is in .bss or the ROM is a negative LONG when it is
    handed out (the records of Iorec() and Bconmap(-2), the BPB, an
    scsidriv handle).  A program that tests such a result for an error code
    will take it for one.  Dosound() takes -1 as the query, not any negative
    address, for the same reason.  #727 brings the rules, with the address
    spaces they are for (#727: falcon-aspace; its section follows).  Static Alt-RAM (CONF_WITH_STATIC_ALT_RAM) is not
    offered with this option: the pool layout of the window has no place
    for it.
  - Hatari's 68030 (WinUAE core, --mmu on) runs it: the regression suite
    passes there (tools/run-hatari-tests.py f030, the hatari-tests.yml job
    falcon-mmu).  So the loading of CRP and TC with the code running through
    the low alias of the ROM, early termination at the root and the second
    level, long-format descriptors with the used and modified bits preset in a
    ROM, and the logical caches (cleared before the MMU is on) work in the
    emulator as the manual says.  Not checked there: the S bit and the cache
    inhibit bit, which Hatari does not make visible to the suite; the first is
    for #727's isolation tests.

Falcon (68030) with an address space for every process, as built by #727
.........................................................................

falcon-aspace_defconfig is falcon-mmu plus CONF_WITH_PROCESS_ASPACES: the
backend is bios/arch/m68k/aspace_hw030.c, the counterpart of aspace_hw.c of
the 68040, with the shape of the plan above.  What is particular to it:

  - The tree of the ROM is the boot tree and the kernel half.  With the option
    every page of it is supervisor-only (atari_mmu.S); aspace_hw_init_root()
    copies its upper 64 entries (the window, 0x80000000 up, and the I/O area
    at 0xff000000) into the root of every address space, so the kernel is the
    same, ROM-resident, in all of them, and the lower 64 are the process's
    own.  The first 32 KiB, the TOS low memory, are in every root as
    supervisor-only pages at their physical addresses; nothing else of the
    RAM is reachable at its physical address any more (the pool of the flat
    image, virt_to_phys() for an address below 0x8000 only).
  - The memory of the kernel and of the programs is all in the window:
    phystop is an address of it (memory.S adds the base), the pool is the
    first part of it and the page allocator (pmem32) has the rest, up to
    the top of the ST-RAM.  TT-RAM is not used (it is not in the allocator's
    range).  CONF_WITH_CARTRIDGE is not offered: a cartridge program runs in
    supervisor mode at the addresses of the ROM port, which no tree maps.
  - Descriptors are long ones with S (the page is the kernel's) and WP, and
    have the used and the modified bit set when they are made: the MMU never
    writes back into a table, which would leave the copy of the descriptor in
    the data cache stale.  The software bit "private page" is bit 0 of the
    page address, which the MMU ignores for 4 KiB pages.
  - A switch is a PMOVE to CRP, PFLUSHA and clearing both caches.  The 030's
    caches are addressed logically, so the same memory is cached under every
    address it is reached by: the window for the kernel, the address of the
    process for the process.  The data cache is therefore cleared on every
    return from a GEMDOS call (bdos/arch/m68k/uspaceasm.S), because the
    kernel writes the buffers of a call through the window; loading code and
    changing a mapping clear both (aspace_hw_code_changed(), aspace_hw_flush()).
  - A bus error is a format A or B frame (bios/arch/m68k/userfault.c).  A fault
    of a data cycle gives its address and size in the frame; the page is
    backed and the RTE runs the cycle again.  A fault of the instruction pipe
    has no address that is worked out: the pages of the next eight bytes after
    the program counter are asked for.  A fault in a read-modify-write cycle
    (TAS, CAS) is not served and ends the process, as FreeMiNT does not serve
    it either (the CPU is not relied on to run the whole cycle again): a
    first touch of lazy memory with TAS is a fault of the process.  The
    mmap suite skips its TAS on a clean file page there (TEST_NO_RMW_FAULTS).
    A misaligned access across the boundary of two lazy pages is served the
    same way (both pages are backed, the RTE runs the access again), but
    Hatari's 68030 does not run it reliably: in a few runs out of two or
    three a bus error comes back with the context of the next interrupt and
    the kernel panics.  The mmap suite does not run test_crossing_accesses()
    there (TEST_NO_CROSSING); #737 checks it with a newer core, and the
    hardware has not been tried.
  - There is no desktop and no VDI for a process (as on the other address-space
    configurations), so the shell is the program EMUCON.TOS or, as the tests
    have it, SHELL.TOS of the boot drive; Srealloc() and the other GEMDOS calls
    the kernel makes are plain calls (bdos/kcall.c).
  - A reset needs the low alias of the ROM, which the trees of the processes do
    not map: machine_reset_prepare() loads the static tree again first.
  - The ROM has no initialised data: .data would be in the ROM image, and a
    static with an initialiser written at run time is a bus error in the first
    process that is created (bios/procmem.c had one).
  - tools/run-hatari-tests.py --mode f030a runs the suite under Hatari's 68030
    (--mmu on) with the harness as the shell (make test-hd-shell): the
    isolation suite, mmap, process_calls and the loaders pass; the hatari-tests
    job is falcon-aspace.  The 68030 itself is checked on Hatari's WinUAE
    core only (#737 covers the correctness fixes of newer ones).
  - The TT (#728): the same image runs on Hatari's TT (--machine tt --cpulevel 3
    --mmu on, 32-bit addressing, 10 MB of ST-RAM and 32 MB of TT-RAM;
    run-hatari-tests.py --mode tta).  TT-RAM is not BDOS alternative RAM there
    (altram_init() is not called: its addresses would be the physical ones, in
    the lower half, which belongs to the processes): pmem32_add() gives its
    pages to the page allocator, and they are reached through the window like
    the ST-RAM ones.  The tree of atari_mmu.S maps the TT-RAM as it is; DMA does
    not use it (the drivers bounce what is outside the ST-RAM of the window).
    Hatari's --conout reads the arguments of the BIOS call from the stack as a
    physical address, which a kernel in the window is not on a TT, so
    run-hatari-tests.py reads the result from the screen (the font of the ROM)
    in that mode.
  - The 68040 (#728): falcon-aspace040_defconfig (CONF_ATARI_HIGHER_HALF_68040)
    is the same ROM for the MMU of a 68040.  startup.S sets TC, ITT/DTT, URP
    and SRP with movec (the 68030 pmove is an F-line trap there), the static
    tree is atari_mmu040.S, and the page tables of the processes are those of
    the backend of virt-m68k, aspace_hw.c.  The 68040 has no early termination,
    so the tree is three full levels and maps only the first 16 MiB, twice
    (at 0 and in the window) with the same tables; the I/O area of the ST
    (0xff000000 and up) is the transparent window DTT0 of the supervisor, the
    part at 0x00f00000 is not cached.  There is no TT-RAM, as the tree does not
    reach it.  The data cache is physical there, so the clearing of it at every
    return to a program (the 68030's, see above) is not done.  Hatari's Falcon
    with --cpulevel 4 --mmu on runs the whole suite (--mode f040a, job
    falcon-aspace040).

virt-m68k, as built by #569 (CONF_WITH_HIGHER_HALF)
....................................................

The image that requires the PMMU (virt-m68k-mmu_defconfig,
CONF_REQUIRE_PMMU) is a higher-half kernel with the 68040 MMU on from the
start.  QEMU's 68040 only: the check of #564 now accepts a 68040 or a 68060,
not a 68030, which has a PMMU but not the tree below.

  - emutos.ld: the TOS low memory -- the vectors, the system variables, the
    BIOS stack and the variables of .low_stram, 32 KiB at most -- stays at its
    addresses, where the absolute short addressing and Setexc() expect it
    (nothing of the m68k code had to change).  The rest of the kernel (.text,
    .data, .bss) is linked in the window, right after it: the RAM at physical 0
    is at 0x80000000, the bytes are loaded at their physical addresses
    (LMA = VMA - 0x80000000), and QEMU's -kernel loader turns the entry point
    into the physical one, as it does on virt-arm.
  - startup.S: main is a trampoline that always continues at the physical
    (low) alias of itself, so that it works both from QEMU's reset (MMU off)
    and from a software reset (warm_reset() jumps to main with the MMU on).
    The code up to the jump to the window is position independent: a symbol
    of the window is converted with PHYS() where it has to be reached before
    the MMU is on (the PMMU check, the bootinfo list QEMU puts after the
    image, the RAM size kept for a software reset).  Then
    virt_mmu_early.S builds the tables and switches the MMU on, and startup.S
    jumps to the window and goes on as before.
  - The tree has one root for the supervisor and the user (URP = SRP), page
    size 4 KiB, with the descriptors global.  The RAM is in it twice, at 0
    (the pool the memory of programs is taken from, the TOS low memory, and
    where the start runs when the MMU is switched on) and at the window, and
    the two share their pointer and page tables.  The pool is the memory
    after the kernel image (virt_pool_start()), so Malloc() and Pexec()
    results stay positive LONGs, and phystop, membot and memtop are pool
    addresses.  The first page (the vectors, the system variables and the
    BIOS stack) is supervisor-only; the rest of the low memory is user
    accessible, as .low_stram is meant to be.  The tables are at the top of
    the RAM, and phystop stops below them (about 1 KiB per MiB of RAM).
    RAM is limited to 1 GiB, what the window holds.
  - The devices (0xff000000 and up) are not in the tree: DTTR0 maps that
    16 MiB to itself, supervisor only and not cached.  Nothing else is
    mapped, and faults.
  - processor_init() leaves the MMU alone (it used to disable translation
    and install transparent translations for all addresses, which would
    take the window away).
  - Addresses given to hardware are converted: virtio (VIRTIO_PHYS(), as on
    virt-arm) and the goldfish TTY's read buffer.  NLS_MSGID_FLAG is
    0xc0000000, as on virt-arm.
  - As on virt-arm, the window is not supervisor-only yet: the command line
    and the desktop run in user mode from the kernel image (#568, #570).

What QEMU's 68040 MMU (QEMU 10.1) does and does not do, as far as this uses
it:

  - The tree walk follows the 68040's three levels, 4 KiB pages, and writes
    the used/modified bits back into the descriptors, which therefore have to
    be in writable RAM.  Table descriptors with UDT = 2 are valid.
  - The S bit of a page is enforced: a user-mode read of the first page ends
    in an access error (a format 7 frame; the BIOS then panics: "Bus Error").
  - DTTR0 works as a transparent window with the supervisor-only (S = 01) and
    cache mode fields; ITTR0/1 and DTTR1 are not needed and are cleared.
  - CPUSHA, PFLUSHA and the cache control register are accepted; there are
    no caches to flush, and no ATC behaviour that differs from the
    architecture was found with a tree that is not changed after TC.E is set
    (what PFLUSH/PFLUSHAN need for the per-process trees is #570's).
  - The 68060 of QEMU has neither TC nor the TTRs (movec raises an illegal
    instruction), and there is no 68030 PMMU: -cpu m68030 is a CPU without
    one, as tools/test-pmmu-check.sh counts it.
  - The ELF loader rewrites e_entry for p_vaddr != p_paddr in the segment
    that holds it (as for virt-arm); the bootinfo list is placed after the
    highest *physical* address of the image.


Raspberry Pi, as built by #567 (CONF_WITH_HIGHER_HALF)
.......................................................

The same design on the Pi 1, 2, 3 and 4, whenever the MMU is set up at all
(CONF_WITH_ARM_PMMU, the default; without it the kernel stays low, as it was).
What differs from virt-arm:

  - The RAM is at physical 0, so the window is the RAM at 0x80000000 + its
    physical address, and the firmware's load address 0x8000 is where the
    kernel is: linked at 0x80008000, the load addresses of the sections are
    the link addresses minus 0x80000000 (emutos.ld, LMA_AT()), so
    "objcopy -O binary" still makes the same kernel.img.  The pool at virtual
    0 is the RAM at its physical address, so it doubles as the mapping that
    the start code and a reset run in; nothing else of the low half is
    mapped.
  - The start (bios/machine/raspi/startup.S up to the jump into the window)
    keeps what the firmware gave it in registers (r0-r2, and whether it was
    started in HYP mode), and uses a small stack just below the kernel.  The
    one C function it calls, raspi_mmu_bootstrap() (raspi_mmu_boot.c), asks
    the VideoCore how much RAM there is (the same property tag as later, with
    the uncached bus alias of a buffer below the kernel), builds both tables
    in the megabyte at the top of the RAM -- TTBR1 16 KiB, TTBR0 8 KiB, the
    page table of the first megabyte of the window for
    CONF_WITH_MMU_TEXT_PROTECT, then the coherent buffers -- and switches the
    MMU on.  startup.S clears the BSS from the window and stores what it kept,
    and phystop, which is the top of the pool, the table megabyte excluded.
    The window is 1 GiB at most (a Pi 4 with more RAM leaves the rest unused);
    what the ARM sees above its RAM and below its peripherals, the memory of
    the VideoCore, is in the window as device memory: the framebuffer address
    the VideoCore hands out is converted with phys_to_window().
  - The peripherals and the per core registers are in the device half, at
    0xf0000000 | (physical & 0x0fffffff) as in the table above (RASPI_DEV_VA()
    in include/machine/raspi/raspi_memmap.h), and raspi_board.io_base and
    local_base are those virtual addresses; the BCM2711 also has its PCIe
    controller at 0xfd500000 mapped.  The PCIe window of the Pi 4 is not
    reachable without LPAE (#76, #276), as before.
  - Everything a bus master is given goes through virt_to_phys()
    (bios/machine/raspi/memory.c) and then phys_to_bus(): raspi_dma_bus() for
    the mailbox buffer, the hardware cursor image and the DWC2 transfers, and
    virt_to_phys() for the xHCI rings, which the PCIe inbound window reaches
    by the physical address.
  - The vector table is the same handler variables as on virt-arm.  A reset
    (warm_reset(), Ctrl-Alt-Del) comes back to startup.S with the MMU on:
    it switches to the physical alias of the code and turns the MMU and the
    caches off, as they are at power on, then starts as the first boot did.
  - The kernel half is not supervisor-only yet, for the same reason as on
    virt-arm (#568).


Per-process address spaces, as built by #568 (ARM) and #570 (m68k)
...................................................................

CONF_WITH_PROCESS_ASPACES (Kconfig.machine) turns on CONF_WITH_USER_ASPACE
on the 32-bit MMU machines, with the generic layer the x32 kernel has:
every process in user mode, in a table of its own, with the kernel half
supervisor-only.  It needs the higher-half kernel and a command line that is
a program (CONF_EXTERNAL_CLI): the AES and the desktop still run from the
kernel image in the kernel's own address space, so the option is off with
CONF_WITH_AES.  The configurations are virt-arm-aspace_defconfig,
rpi2-aspace_defconfig and virt-m68k-aspace_defconfig, tested by CI as the
regression machines virt-arm-aspace, raspi2b-aspace and virt-m68k-aspace
(tools/run-regression-tests.sh).

The isolation suite (tests/isolation/, #572) is the ARM and m68k counterpart of
x32's ring-3 probe, and runs in those three regression machines.  A program,
ISOPROBE.TOS, makes one forbidden access per launch and the suite checks what
becomes of it: a read, a write or a jump to the kernel half, to the low
memory and to an unmapped address, and a privileged instruction, each end the
process with a fault (exit code 0xffff) and leave no process record or memory
behind; the private pages of the parent (image, stack, heap, basepage) are
neither seen nor changed from the child; and the calls that take a pointer
refuse one into the kernel with the documented error (GEMDOS and Rwabs()
EIMBA; Setpalette(), Getmpb(), Iorec(), Kbdvbase(), Dosound(), Setexc() and
Super() EACCDN).  Checked by hand: with the kernel half of virt-arm made
accessible to user mode the suite fails on the first read of it.

What is shared by both architectures:

  - bios/pmem32.c is a bitmap page allocator for the RAM after the kernel
    image and the pool of the GEMDOS memory descriptors (the pool is a quarter
    of the RAM, 1 to 8 MiB, in the window; bios/biosmem.c).  Everything a
    process owns -- its pages, its page tables, its kernel stack -- and the
    kernel heap (bios/kheap.c) come from it.  The pool of the memory
    descriptors is in the kernel half now: no address in the lower half is
    the kernel's, and the TPA of a process is a block of procmem's window,
    0x00200000, 2 MiB (bios/procmem.c), that is mapped into its address space.
  - bios/aspace.c is the address space: it owns the root and the table pages
    the backend asks for, maps private pages (freed with the space) and
    borrowed ones (procmem blocks), and destroys all of it together.  What an
    architecture provides is include/aspace32.h; bios/progimage.c validates
    and maps the layout of a program (segments in USER_IMAGE_BASE..+4 MiB,
    none sharing a page).
  - Each process has a kernel stack of 4 pages.  Exceptions from user mode,
    GEMDOS, BIOS and XBIOS calls run on it, and gouser() (Pexec()) waits on the
    stack of the launcher with a setjmp(); the process that ends, by Pterm()
    or by a fault, returns to it by longjmp() (bdos/arch/<arch>/uspace.c).
    The kernel calls GEMDOS functions directly (CONF_WITH_KCALL, bdos/kcall.c).
  - A call from user mode is checked (bdos/uaccess.c, bios/biosaccess.c,
    bios/usertrap.c) against the pages of the caller before it runs.
    Super() is refused except Super(1); Supexec() and TRAP #2 (the AES and VDI)
    are refused; the Line-A is a fault.  A fault ends that process only
    (proc_fault()).  Disk I/O to a user buffer goes through a bounce buffer
    (blkdev_rwabs()), as DMA wants physical addresses.
  - A program in ELF finds its stack private and far from its TPA (the stack top
    is 0x80000000 - 8): libcmini's startup keeps a stack that is not in the TPA
    and does not shrink it (lib/libcmini, crt0 and main.c); a classic PRG on the
    68k is started the TOS way, below.  EmuCON for such a
    kernel is built with CLI_SSYSTEM=1 (cli/Makefile): it asks Ssystem() for
    what it read from the system variables and the cookie jar.  A program must
    not be linked below 32 KiB (m68k: the kernel's low memory is there) and no
    two of its segments may share a page.

ARM (virt-arm, Raspberry Pi 2 and up; the Pi 1 builds, ARMv6):

  - TTBCR.N = 1; TTBR1 is the kernel half, supervisor-only (AP privileged
    access only), the same for the whole uptime; TTBR0 is the root of the
    process, 8 KiB aligned to 8 KiB (bios/arch/arm/aspace_hw.c).  The second
    level is Linux-style: a 4 KiB page holds the hardware tables of two
    neighbouring megabytes and, in the other half, a software word per page
    (the OWNED flag).
  - Every process runs with ASID 0; a switch (Pexec(), Pterm()) loads TTBR0
    and invalidates by ASID, the global entries of the kernel half stay.
  - The GEMDOS trap is SVC 1 (bdos/arch/arm/uspaceasm.S): the frame of the
    caller -- the registers and the FPU state -- is saved on the kernel stack,
    and returned from with all the registers restored, so nothing of the kernel
    is left in them.  Faults in user mode take the way of any_vec() to
    proc_fault().  BIOS and XBIOS calls (vectorsasm.S) check their arguments
    with bios_user_check().
  - The Raspberry Pi additionally builds both tables at boot as for #567
    (RASPI_TTBR_MODE) and a reset loads the boot table again
    (machine_reset_prepare()).

m68k (virt-m68k, 68040; the 68060 is the same tree, untested as QEMU's has no
MMU registers):

  - The tree is the three levels of the 68040 with 4 KiB pages
    (bios/arch/m68k/aspace_hw.c).  The root is 128 entries of 32 MiB; URP and
    SRP both point to the root of the running process.  Entries 64-127 (the
    kernel half) are copied from the boot tree into every root, so they are
    the same pointer tables everywhere, and entries 0-63 are the process's
    own.  The tables are 512 and 256 bytes and are cut from the pages the
    core hands out (the first from the page of the root).
  - The kernel's low memory -- the vectors, the system variables, the BIOS
    stack and the variables of .low_stram, 32 KiB -- is at address 0 in every
    root, supervisor-only, through one page table that all the roots share.
  - A switch writes URP and SRP and does PFLUSHAN; the kernel's pages are
    global (G).  The caches are written back and invalidated (CPUSHA) when
    code was written, and the tree is flushed with PFLUSHA before a reset
    (machine_reset_prepare() loads the boot tree: the start code of a reset
    runs at the physical address).
  - The trap #1 entry (bdos/arch/m68k/uspaceasm.S) saves d0-d7/a0-a6 and USP on
    the kernel stack, copies the words of the call from the user stack (what is
    not readable reads as 0, so that the kernel never touches an unmapped
    address), and gives osif() the packed frame the 68000 would.  Trap #13 and
    #14 (vectors.S, bios/arch/m68k/m68kusertrap.c) decode the packed words of
    the calls that take pointers into the long arguments the checks are
    written for, and run on the kernel stack too, unlike the classic handler
    which runs the BIOS on the stack of its caller.  Any exception frame with
    the S bit clear ends the process (any_vec() -> m68k_user_fault()).
  - A classic PRG keeps the TOS contract (KPROC_LEGACY_PRG, bdos/kproc.h): it is
    loaded into a TPA of the window that is the free memory (at most 1 MiB, half
    of the window, so that its children have room), it starts with its stack
    pointer at the top of the TPA (a return address of 0 and the basepage at
    4(sp), as TOS gives it) and chooses its own stack, and Mshrink() of its
    basepage takes back what it does not need, in whole pages: they are
    unmapped from its address space and from the loan its launcher holds, then
    given back to the window (kproc_shrink_tpa()).  p_hitpa stays what it was.
    A legacy startup that sets its stack from p_hitpa, shrinks and runs needs
    nothing else.  The program is still in an address space of its own: what is
    not its TPA, its Malloc() memory or its image is not there, and Super(),
    Supexec(), the system variables and the hardware are as above.
  - A program in ELF is started the way ARM and x32 programs are: with a private
    stack (0x7ffffff0, a return address of 0 and the basepage at 4(sp)), an
    image of its own, and a TPA that is no more than its basepage.  libcmini
    tells the two apart by where the stack pointer is at the start, in crt0 and
    _crtinit(): in the TPA, it is the classic start; outside it, the private
    one.
  - Not done: the 68060 (#707), the floppy calls, FPU state across
    a Pexec(), and the real-hardware cache details (the cache modes of the
    tables are copyback; the 68060's branch cache is not flushed).


Kernel pointers and LONG results
---------------------------------

Every kernel address is negative as a LONG.  TOS error codes are negative
too, and the top of the device window (0xffffff00 and up) is
indistinguishable from them.  So no BIOS, XBIOS or GEMDOS result handed to
a user may hold a kernel address, and neither may a structure or variable a
user process can read (Iorec(), Kbdvbase(), Getbpb(), the cookie jar,
Ssystem()).  doc/kernel-pointers.txt lists every such place with its
decision; #563 implements them in the shared code, which the MMU entries
call as the x32 one does.
